# Fitness coaching workflow

Use this workflow for complete programs and substantial revisions. It is designed for general-wellness coaching of adults, not clinical nutrition or medical exercise prescription.

## Contents

- 1. Select the right response depth
- 2. Screen before personalizing
- 3. Gather a practical profile
- 4. Build the workout plan
- 5. Build nutrition and diet options
- 6. Check in and adjust
- 7. Final review checklist

## 1. Select the right response depth

Choose the smallest path that satisfies the request:

| Request | Gather | Return |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One meal, workout, or swap | The goal, relevant restrictions, time, equipment, and immediate context | A few safe, feasible options and one next action |
| Training-only plan | Goal, experience, schedule, equipment, activity, recovery, and safety screen | A progressive weekly schedule with substitutions |
| Nutrition-only plan | Goal, restrictions, preferred foods/cuisines, budget, cooking constraints, and safety screen | Flexible meal templates or a meal plan with swaps |
| Combined program | All relevant training and nutrition inputs | A coordinated training, food, recovery, and check-in plan |
| Check-in or plateau | What was planned, what happened, adherence, recovery, pain, and barriers | One explained adjustment rather than a wholesale reset |

Do not turn a focused request into an unnecessary long plan. Do not use an intake form as a reason to delay simple, safe help.

## 2. Screen before personalizing

The skill serves generally healthy adults aged 18 and over. The referral conditions are listed in step 1 of SKILL.md and apply to every request, including one-off questions.

Before a complete plan, ask this screen once as a short yes/no group, and do not ask for details on a “no”. It follows the seven general-health questions of the [PAR-Q+](https://eparmedx.com/) readiness questionnaire and adds the pregnancy, surgery, and eating-disorder items that this skill's scope also excludes.

1. Has a doctor ever said you have a heart condition or high blood pressure?
2. Do you feel chest pain at rest, in daily life, or during activity?
3. Do you lose your balance from dizziness, or have you lost consciousness in the past 12 months?
4. Do you have any other diagnosed chronic condition, or take prescription medication for one?
5. Do you have a bone, joint, or muscle problem, or pain, now or in the past 12 months, that activity could make worse?
6. Has a doctor ever said you should exercise only under medical supervision?
7. Are you pregnant, breastfeeding, recovering from surgery, or in rehabilitation?
8. For nutrition or weight-loss requests only: has disordered eating, or a difficult relationship with food or exercise, ever been a concern for you?

Treat a “yes” to any question as a referral condition. A “yes” to 2 or 3 is also a symptom.

For an urgent symptom, advise urgent local medical care. For non-urgent out-of-scope cases, offer to help the user prepare questions for their clinician or registered dietitian. Do not make a plan “with a disclaimer” after identifying a referral condition.

## 3. Gather a practical profile

Ask only for missing inputs that change the requested plan. Keep questions in small logical groups.

### Essential for a combined plan

| Area | Ask for | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Goal | Primary goal, why it matters, target date, and success measure | Do not promise a result by the date. |
| Training | Experience, current activity, enjoyable or disliked activities, and recent consistency | “Beginner,” “intermediate,” and “advanced” are useful only with context. |
| Logistics | Available days, session duration, preferred training times, equipment, access, travel or shift work | Fit the actual week first. |
| Limits | Safety screen, movement limitations, recovery, sleep, and stress constraints | Do not prescribe around pain or injury. |
| Food | Dietary restrictions, allergies, foods to avoid, meal frequency, cooking skill, equipment, food access, budget, and shopping cadence | A restriction always wins over a food suggestion. |
| Food culture | Cuisines, staple foods, religious practices, dishes the user already cooks, and desired level of authenticity | Ethnicity is optional context, never a required input or a food preference proxy. |

### Helpful only when relevant

- Age, height, weight, sex-related physiology if the user wants energy or macro estimates.
- Current calorie/macro tracking, if the user already tracks and wants help interpreting the plan.
- Household size, leftovers, restaurant meals, and refrigeration/freezer access for meal prep.
- Training performance markers, such as comfortable loads, walking pace, or running distance, for progression.

If a user does not want to share body measurements, use food patterns, portion templates, training performance, and habit goals instead. Do not pressure them to disclose sensitive data.

## 4. Build the workout plan

### Planning rules

1. Match the plan to the primary goal and actual constraints.
2. Start below the maximum sustainable workload, especially for beginners and people returning after inactivity.
3. Cover appropriate movement patterns over the week, using exercises the user can safely perform with their equipment.
4. Include a warm-up, effort guidance, rest, recovery, and simple form cues.
5. Use a gradual progression. Increase one variable at a time, such as reps, load, range of motion, sets, pace, or duration.
6. Provide substitutions that preserve the session’s purpose when equipment is limited or an exercise is disliked.
7. Avoid max-effort testing, advanced techniques, and unnecessary periodization unless the user’s experience and goal justify them.

For general health, established U.S. guidance encourages adults to move more, build toward regular aerobic activity, and include muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days weekly. Use that as broad context, not as a quota that overrides the user’s starting point or capacity. [Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans](https://odphp.health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/about-physical-activity-guidelines/questions-answers)

### Effort cues

Give every set or interval an effort cue the user can judge without equipment.

- **Strength:** reps in reserve (RIR), the clean reps left when a set ends. Stabilization and endurance work, typical for beginners, sits at about 3–5 in reserve, and hypertrophy blocks mostly at 1–3. New trainees tend to overestimate how close to failure they are, so for beginners lead with “stop while technique is still clean” and treat RIR as a secondary cue. A set also ends when form breaks, whatever the target was. [NASM: reps in reserve](https://www.nasm.org/resource-center/blog/training/reps-in-reserve-coaching-intensity-with-rir)
- **Cardio:** the talk test. Moderate effort means you can talk but not sing (about 5–6 on a 0–10 scale). Vigorous effort means you cannot say more than a few words without pausing for breath (about 7–8). [CDC: measuring intensity](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/measuring/index.html)

### Workout output format

For each session, include:

- Session name, purpose, approximate duration, and equipment.
- A short warm-up.
- Exercise, sets, reps or time, effort cue, rest, and one concise form cue.
- Optional substitute(s) when useful.
- Cool-down or recovery action only if it fits the session.

Then include a weekly progression rule, for example: “Work within a rep range, such as 8–12. If all working sets felt controlled, met the effort cue, and kept consistent technique for two sessions, add one or two repetitions next time. Once every set reaches the top of the range, add the smallest available load and return to the bottom of the range. Otherwise repeat the session.” Do not prescribe progression through pain.

## 5. Build nutrition and diet options

### Principles

- Start with food the user already enjoys and can obtain, prepare, and afford.
- Offer dietary patterns as choices, not moral rankings: for example, omnivore, pescatarian, vegetarian, vegan, halal, kosher, or cuisine-centered meal templates when the user asks for them.
- Prefer flexible meal templates and like-for-like substitutions over a rigid universal menu.
- Include a range of nutrient-dense, minimally processed foods while respecting cultural staples and convenience needs.
- Never use shame, “good” versus “bad” food labels, detox claims, or rapid body-change promises.

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030 emphasize whole, nutrient-dense foods and protein foods at every meal. They are a general reference, not a substitute for individualized medical nutrition care. [Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA_508.pdf)

### Calories and macros

Only estimate energy or macro needs when the user wants them and has supplied enough relevant information. Confirm the user's units, convert weight to kilograms before applying any per-kilogram figure, and show the arithmetic. When providing an estimate:

1. Name the inputs and method in plain language. Default to the Mifflin–St Jeor equation for resting energy, multiplied by an activity factor. It landed within 10% of measured resting metabolic rate for more adults than the other common equations, though it can still miss for an individual ([Frankenfield et al., 2005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jada.2005.02.005)).
2. Present a range or a rounded starting point, not a precise guarantee.
3. State that restaurant meals, cooking fats, portion variation, activity, and individual differences create uncertainty.
4. Pair the number with non-numeric options, such as protein-containing meals, fruit and vegetables, fiber-rich staples, hydration, regular meals, or meal-prep structure.
5. Never suggest a very-low-calorie diet, make a disease-specific target, or imply that a number is clinically appropriate. Keep any deficit or surplus small and gradual, do not propose an intake below the user's estimated resting energy needs, and treat a request for less as a request for severe restriction under section 2.
6. For protein, a common planning range for adults who train regularly is about 1.2–2.0 g/kg per day. The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines give 1.2–1.6 g/kg, adjusted to individual caloric requirements ([DGA](https://cdn.realfood.gov/DGA_508.pdf)), and the ISSN position stand gives 1.4–2.0 g/kg for most exercising individuals ([Jäger et al., 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5477153/)). Stay near the lower end for general fitness, use the upper end only for a resistance-training or muscle-gain goal, and express the target as meals and foods the user already eats, not supplements.

If exact nutrition information matters, encourage checking a reputable food database, packaging label, or registered dietitian. Label every quantity not supplied by the user or a reliable source as an estimate.

### Cuisine-aware adaptation

When adapting a plan:

1. Ask for the cuisine, dishes, ingredients, and preparation styles the user actually wants.
2. Preserve familiar staples where possible.
3. Give substitutions based on local availability, time, and the dietary restriction.
4. Do not caricature a cuisine or imply that an ethnicity determines eating habits.
5. If converting a familiar household portion into a gram amount, say that it is an estimate and offer a household-measure alternative.

### Nutrition output format

Unless the user asks for a full meal schedule, lead with:

- The food principles that support the stated goal.
- Two to four meal or snack templates using preferred foods.
- At least one restriction-safe or convenience-oriented swap where relevant.
- A concise grocery, prep, or restaurant strategy if useful.

For a full meal plan, include meal names, ingredients or portions, preparation time, substitutions, and a grocery list. Keep repetition intentional and practical. Make calories or macros optional and clearly estimated.

## 6. Check in and adjust

At a regular check-in, ask for only the signals that inform the next change:

- Sessions completed and perceived difficulty.
- Energy, sleep, hunger, soreness, pain, stress, and enjoyment. When the plan involves weight loss or a large training increase, also ask about recurrent illness and, for users who menstruate, missed or irregular periods. With falling performance or restrictive eating these can signal low energy availability and are referral signs ([summary of the 2023 IOC consensus on REDs](https://pinesnutrition.org/2023-ioc-consensus-statement-on-reds-whats-new/)).
- What was easy, what was difficult, and the practical barrier.
- Any goal, schedule, equipment, food-access, or restriction change.

Adjust one meaningful variable at a time and explain the reason. Examples:

- Fewer sessions or less volume when adherence or recovery is poor.
- A different exercise when equipment access or enjoyment is the blocker.
- Simpler meal templates or batch-prep when cooking time is the blocker.
- More flexible snack or meal swaps when hunger disrupts consistency.

Pause the plan and refer if a check-in reveals a safety issue or an out-of-scope condition.

End a complete plan with a snapshot the user can paste into a later check-in: goal and date, schedule and equipment, restrictions and flagged limits, current loads or portions, and the progression rule. It keeps a later check-in from restarting the intake.

## 7. Final review checklist

Before delivering a complete plan, verify:

- [ ] The user is within the healthy-adult scope.
- [ ] The plan reflects every stated goal, time limit, equipment limit, restriction, preference, and food-access constraint.
- [ ] No diet or cuisine was inferred from ethnicity.
- [ ] Every exercise is appropriate to the stated experience and available equipment, with recovery built in.
- [ ] Every food option respects exclusions; serious allergy or medical questions were not handled as routine substitutions.
- [ ] Calculations and portions are labeled as estimates where needed.
- [ ] There are no medical, supplement, or guaranteed-result claims.
- [ ] The plan gives a realistic first action and names what to report at the next check-in.

## Design sources

The workflow draws prompt-design patterns from structured workout/nutrition intake, modular meal-plan adaptations, and iterative check-ins. Useful examples include [workout and meal-plan prompt patterns](https://www.fitbudd.com/insights/ai-for-personal-trainers), [a modular meal-planning workflow](https://coachway.io/articles/how-to-use-ai-for-meal-planning-as-a-coach/), and research on conversationally refining personalized exercise plans ([PlanFitting](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12555)). Open-source coaching skills informed two additions: the pasteable plan snapshot, adapted from the persistent-profile pattern in [neilberget/fitness-skill](https://github.com/neilberget/fitness-skill), and Mifflin–St Jeor as the default estimator, also used by [Builtbyaran/Personal-trainer-skill](https://github.com/Builtbyaran/Personal-trainer-skill).
